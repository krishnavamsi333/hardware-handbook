Voltage, Current & Power

Every circuit analysis starts here. Voltage, current, and power are the three quantities you will measure, calculate, and reason about on every board you design.

Voltage (V)

Voltage is the electric potential difference between two points. It is the "pressure" that drives current through a circuit. Voltage is always measured between two nodes — it is never an absolute value, always relative.

V = E / Q

Energy (joules) per unit charge (coulombs). Unit: Volt (V)

Practical tip When probing with a multimeter, always ask "voltage with respect to what?" Your black probe defines the reference. Floating the black probe gives meaningless readings.

Current (I)

Current is the flow of electric charge through a conductor. It is measured in Amperes (A), where 1 A = 1 coulomb of charge passing a point per second.

I = Q / t

Charge (coulombs) per unit time (seconds). Unit: Ampere (A)

Practical tip Current must flow in a loop. If you cannot trace a complete path from source back to source, current cannot flow — no matter how much voltage is applied.

Power (P)

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or dissipated. In electronics, power dissipated as heat is what destroys components — always check power budgets.

P = V × I

P = I² × R

P = V² / R

Unit: Watt (W). All three forms are equivalent via Ohm's Law.

Practical tip When selecting resistors, calculate the power dissipation first. A 100 Ω resistor with 5 V across it dissipates 250 mW — a standard 1/4 W resistor is already at its limit. Use the I²R form when current is known; use V²/R when voltage is fixed.

Energy (E)

Energy is power integrated over time. Batteries and capacitors store energy, not power. This is the quantity relevant for battery sizing and runtime calculations.

E = P × t = V × I × t

Unit: Joule (J), or Watt-hour (Wh) for battery sizing. 1 Wh = 3600 J

Common Mistakes

  • Measuring voltage with the multimeter in current mode — this shorts the circuit through the meter's shunt and can blow the fuse.
  • Ignoring power dissipation when choosing resistors — always derate to 50% of rated power in continuous operation.
  • Confusing energy and power — a battery's capacity in mAh is a charge quantity, not directly power. Multiply by voltage to get energy (Wh).
