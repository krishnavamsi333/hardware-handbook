Fundamentals

Pull-ups & Pull-downs

A pull-up or pull-down resistor gives a digital line a known, stable state when nothing else is actively driving it. Without one, "undriven" doesn't mean 0 — it means undefined.

Why They Exist

Many digital outputs (open-drain, open-collector, tri-state buses, switches, buttons) can only actively drive one logic level, or sometimes drive nothing at all. A bare wire connected to nothing — or to a high-impedance input — will float to whatever voltage is induced by noise, capacitive coupling, or leakage currents. A resistor to the supply rail (pull-up) or to ground (pull-down) "weakly" forces the line to a known level until something actively overrides it.

Pull-up Resistor

A resistor connected between the signal line and VCC. When nothing pulls the line low, it sits at VCC. When a switch, button, or open-drain output pulls it to ground, current flows through the resistor and the line drops to a low logic level.

I = VCC / Rpullup  (current when line is pulled low)

This current is wasted as heat whenever the line is held low — a key reason for choosing the resistor value carefully.

Pull-down Resistor

The mirror image: a resistor between the signal line and ground. The line idles low, and an active-high driver or switch to VCC pulls it high. Pull-downs are common on enable pins that must default to "disabled" or "safe" if the driving signal is not yet present (e.g. during power-up before an MCU's GPIO is configured).

Choosing a Value

The resistor value is a trade-off between several competing concerns:

  • Too low (e.g. 1 kΩ): Strong, noise-resistant, fast rise/fall time — but draws more current whenever the line is asserted, and a driver pulling against it must sink/source more current.
  • Too high (e.g. 100 kΩ–1 MΩ): Very low power draw, but slow rise time when combined with line capacitance (RC time constant), and more susceptible to noise pulling the "undriven" level away from its intended value.

trise ≈ R × Cline  (time to reach ~63% of VCC)

A 10 kΩ pull-up against 20 pF of bus capacitance gives τ = 200 ns — fine for a slow GPIO, far too slow for a fast I²C or SPI bus.

General-purpose GPIO input10 kΩGood balance of speed and power

Internal vs External Pull Resistors

Most microcontrollers include weak internal pull-up or pull-down resistors (typically 20–100 kΩ) that can be enabled in software on GPIO pins. These are convenient for buttons and simple sensing, but:

  • They are too weak and too imprecise for fast buses like I²C — use external resistors there.
  • Their value can vary significantly across temperature and supply voltage, and between vendors.
  • They draw current the moment the pin configuration is applied, even before firmware "settles" — worth checking against power budgets in sleep modes.
Practical tip If a GPIO controls something safety-relevant (an enable pin on a motor driver, a relay, an H-bridge shutdown), never rely on an internal pull resistor alone for the default state. Use an external resistor sized so the pin defaults to the safe state even if the MCU is unprogrammed, in reset, or its firmware hangs.

Common Mistakes

  • Pulling both ways at once: A pull-up and pull-down on the same line forms a permanent voltage divider — the line never reaches a clean high or low, and both resistors dissipate power continuously.
  • Forgetting the open-drain side: An open-drain or open-collector output with no pull-up anywhere on the bus will never produce a valid "high" — the line floats.
  • Using a pull-down on an active-low enable: If a chip's enable pin is active-low (enabled when pulled to ground) but you add a pull-down, the device is enabled by default — often the opposite of the safe state.
  • Sizing for one bus speed, reusing for another: A 10 kΩ I²C pull-up that worked at 100 kHz may be far too weak (slow) at 400 kHz with the same bus capacitance.
