Digital Logic Levels

"0" and "1" are abstractions. In real hardware, a logic level is a voltage range, and two chips only communicate reliably if their voltage ranges agree — or are deliberately translated.

The Four Key Thresholds

Every digital input and output is specified with four voltage thresholds:

SymbolMeaning
VOHMinimum voltage guaranteed when an output drives "high"
VOLMaximum voltage guaranteed when an output drives "low"
VIHMinimum voltage an input is guaranteed to recognize as "high"
VILMaximum voltage an input is guaranteed to recognize as "low"

For correct communication, an output's guaranteed levels must fall safely inside the receiving input's recognized ranges: VOH ≥ VIH and VOL ≤ VIL. The gap between VIL and VIH is the "forbidden zone" — a voltage in that range is not guaranteed to be read as either 0 or 1.

Noise Margin

Noise margin is the headroom between what an output guarantees and what an input requires — it's the amount of noise a signal can tolerate before it risks being misread.

NMH = VOH − VIH  (high-level noise margin)

NML = VIL − VOL  (low-level noise margin)

Larger noise margins mean a more robust system — important for long cables, noisy environments (near motors, switching supplies), or signals that pass through connectors.

Exact thresholds vary by part and supply voltage — always check the specific datasheet, especially VIH(min) as a fraction of VCC (commonly 0.7 × VCC for CMOS).

Mixed-Voltage Interfacing

A very common real-world scenario: a 3.3 V microcontroller needs to talk to a 5 V peripheral, or vice versa. Whether a level shifter is required depends on direction and tolerance:

  • 3.3 V output → 5 V input: A 3.3 V VOH (~3.0 V minimum) is often, but not always, above the 5 V part's VIH(min) (often 2.0 V for TTL-level inputs, but can be higher for CMOS-level 5 V inputs ~3.5 V). Check the specific VIH — this direction sometimes works without translation, sometimes doesn't.
  • 5 V output → 3.3 V input: A 5 V VOH (~4.0–5 V) will exceed the absolute maximum voltage rating of most 3.3 V inputs (often VCC + 0.3 V = 3.6 V). This direction almost always requires a level shifter or resistor divider — driving a 3.3 V input pin with 5 V can permanently damage it.
  • Open-drain signals (I²C): can often cross voltage domains simply by choosing which rail the pull-up resistor connects to — see the Open-Drain & Open-Collector chapter.
Practical tip "5 V tolerant" inputs exist on many 3.3 V parts — check the datasheet's absolute maximum ratings table, not just the recommended operating conditions. A pin can be specified to operate at 3.3 V logic levels while still surviving (but not necessarily correctly interpreting) a 5 V input, which is a different and more limited guarantee than true 5 V-tolerant logic.

Why This Matters for Debugging

A huge fraction of "intermittent" digital communication problems trace back to logic levels: a signal that sits right at the edge of VIH will be read correctly most of the time but flip occasionally when temperature, supply voltage, or loading shifts the threshold slightly. When a digital signal misbehaves, measuring its actual high and low voltages with a scope or meter — and comparing them against the receiving device's datasheet thresholds — is one of the highest-value first steps.
