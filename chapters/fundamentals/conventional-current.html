Conventional Current

Current direction is a convention — and it is opposite to the actual movement of electrons. This causes confusion every time, so let's settle it clearly.

What is Conventional Current?

Before electrons were discovered, physicists assumed positive charges were the moving carriers. They defined current as flowing from the positive terminal of a source, through the circuit, to the negative terminal. This became conventional current.

In reality, electrons (negative charges) flow from negative to positive — the opposite direction. But conventional current direction is still used universally in circuit analysis, schematics, and datasheet specifications.

Electron Flow vs. Conventional Current

Direction Carrier Used in
Conventional Current + to − (outside source) Positive charge (notional) Schematics, circuit analysis, datasheets
Electron Flow − to + (outside source) Electrons (actual) Physics, semiconductor discussions
Practical tip Always use conventional current when reading datasheets and drawing schematics. Diode forward current, transistor collector/base/emitter currents, and all component ratings are specified in conventional current direction. Thinking in electron flow will give you the wrong polarity.

Why It Still Matters

In semiconductors, both electrons and holes carry current. Holes are positive charge carriers that move in the conventional current direction. So in a P-type semiconductor, conventional current direction actually matches the physical motion of the dominant carriers (holes).

For day-to-day hardware work, the rule is simple: follow conventional current. Current flows out of the positive supply rail, through your load, and returns to the negative rail (ground).

Diode Example

A diode conducts when conventional current flows from anode (+) to cathode (−). The schematic arrow on a diode symbol points in the direction of conventional current flow. The cathode bar marks where conventional current exits.

Anode (+) → [Diode] → Cathode (−)

Conventional current flows in the direction the diode symbol's arrow points.

Practical tip When placing protection diodes, flyback diodes, or TVS diodes — always verify polarity using conventional current direction. The cathode connects to the higher-potential rail.
