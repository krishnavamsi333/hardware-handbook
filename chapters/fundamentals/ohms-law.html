Ohm's Law

The single most-used equation in electronics. If you understand Ohm's Law in your bones — not just as a formula — you will catch design errors before they happen.

The Law

For a resistive element, the voltage across it is directly proportional to the current through it. The constant of proportionality is resistance.

V = I × R

I = V / R

R = V / I

V in Volts, I in Amperes, R in Ohms (Ω)

Intuition

Think of it as a water analogy:

  • Voltage = water pressure
  • Current = flow rate
  • Resistance = pipe constriction

More pressure (voltage) drives more flow (current) through the same pipe. A narrower pipe (higher resistance) reduces flow for the same pressure.

Worked Examples

LED Current Limiting Resistor

You have a 5 V supply, an LED with a forward voltage of 2 V, and you want 10 mA through it. The resistor must drop: 5 V − 2 V = 3 V.

R = V / I = 3 V / 0.01 A = 300 Ω

Use a standard 330 Ω (next value up). Power: P = I²R = (0.01)² × 330 = 33 mW → safe for 1/4 W resistor.

Voltage Drop on a Power Rail

A 3.3 V rail carries 500 mA through PCB traces with 0.2 Ω total resistance.

V_drop = I × R = 0.5 A × 0.2 Ω = 100 mV

The load sees 3.2 V, not 3.3 V. This matters for tight-tolerance devices.

Where Ohm's Law Doesn't Apply

Ohm's Law applies to linear, resistive elements only. The following are non-ohmic:

  • Diodes — exponential V-I relationship
  • Transistors — controlled current sources
  • Capacitors — V-I relationship depends on frequency (I = C × dV/dt)
  • Inductors — V-I relationship depends on rate of change (V = L × dI/dt)
Practical tip Ohm's Law is your first sanity check. Whenever a circuit behaves unexpectedly, apply V = IR to each element and verify the numbers make sense. Most "mysterious" failures are just Ohm's Law being violated by a wrong resistor value, a wrong assumption about current, or a ground not connected.
