Fundamentals

Push-Pull Outputs

The default output stage on almost every digital pin. Two transistors, one job each: one "pushes" the line to VCC, the other "pulls" it to GND. Together they can drive a line to either state quickly and with low impedance.

How It Works

A push-pull stage is built from a complementary pair of switches — typically a P-channel MOSFET (or PNP transistor) on top, connected to VCC, and an N-channel MOSFET (or NPN transistor) on the bottom, connected to GND. Exactly one of the pair is on at a time:

  • Output high: top transistor ON, bottom OFF — pin connected to VCC through a low-resistance path.
  • Output low: bottom transistor ON, top OFF — pin connected to GND through a low-resistance path.

Because the pin is always actively connected to one rail or the other, push-pull outputs have fast, symmetric rise and fall times and a well-defined output impedance — no external pull resistor is needed for the logic levels themselves.

Output Impedance and Drive Strength

A push-pull output isn't a perfect voltage source — each transistor has an on-resistance (RDS(on) for MOSFETs), which determines how much the output voltage sags under load.

VOUT = VCC − Iload × RDS(on)  (driving high)

A microcontroller pin with RDS(on) = 30 Ω driving 8 mA will sag by 240 mV from the rail — check this against your receiver's VIH minimum.

Datasheets specify maximum source/sink current per pin (commonly a few mA to tens of mA for MCU GPIO) and total current limits per package — exceeding either can cause excessive voltage sag, overheating, or permanent damage.

The "Drive Fight" Problem

Because both transistors actively drive the line, two push-pull outputs must never be connected directly together unless both are guaranteed to always agree on the output state. If one drives high while the other drives low, a low-impedance path forms directly from VCC to GND through both output stages — a near short circuit.

  • This is why shared buses (I²C, shared resets, shared interrupt lines) use open-drain outputs instead.
  • It's also why UART TX lines, SPI MOSI/MISO, and other point-to-point signals are safe as push-pull — each line has exactly one driver.
  • Tri-state (three-state) buffers solve this for buses that genuinely need multiple push-pull-capable drivers (e.g. parallel memory buses): each driver can electrically disconnect itself (high-Z) when not addressed.
Practical tip During bring-up, if you reconfigure a pin's direction in firmware (e.g. switching a pin between input and output, or between two peripherals), make sure both ends of a connection are never simultaneously configured as push-pull outputs with different intended states — even momentarily during a GPIO reconfiguration sequence. Brief drive fights during mode transitions are a common source of mysterious glitches and, over time, damaged pins.

Push-Pull vs Open-Drain — When to Use Which

SituationRecommended Output Type
Single driver, single receiver (UART, SPI, point-to-point GPIO)Push-pull
Shared bus with multiple potential drivers (I²C, shared IRQ/reset)Open-drain
Driving an LED or low-current load directlyPush-pull
Interfacing between different voltage domains on a shared lineOpen-drain
Status/fault flag that multiple ICs may assertOpen-drain

Driving Larger Loads

A push-pull GPIO can directly drive small loads (a few mA — an LED with a series resistor, a logic-level input on another chip). For anything larger — relays, motors, high-current LEDs, solenoids — the GPIO drives an external switch (a MOSFET or driver IC) that itself forms a push-pull or open-drain stage at a higher power level. The GPIO's job becomes controlling the switch, not being the switch.
