Fundamentals

Open-Drain & Open-Collector

An open-drain (MOSFET) or open-collector (BJT) output can only pull a line toward one rail — usually ground. It can never drive the line high. That single restriction is exactly what makes shared buses and mixed-voltage interfacing possible.

How It Works

In a normal push-pull output, the driver actively connects the pin to either VCC or GND. In an open-drain output, the "high side" transistor is simply not there. The output stage is a single transistor whose drain (or collector) is connected to the pin, and whose source (or emitter) goes to ground.

  • Transistor ON: pin is pulled to GND (logic low)
  • Transistor OFF: pin is left floating (high impedance) — neither high nor low

To get a usable "high" level, an external (or sometimes internal) pull-up resistor is required, connected from the pin to VCC. When the transistor is off, the pull-up resistor pulls the line to VCC.

Why Use It

1. Multiple Drivers on One Line (Wired-AND)

Any number of open-drain outputs can be tied to the same line with a single shared pull-up. If any device pulls the line low, the line is low. The line is high only when every device releases it. This is exactly the behavior needed for:

  • I²C bus — both SDA and SCL are open-drain so multiple masters and slaves can share the bus without conflict, and clock stretching works naturally.
  • Shared interrupt lines — several peripherals can assert a common IRQ line; the MCU sees "low" if any one of them needs attention.
  • SMBus alerts, reset lines shared across multiple ICs — any device can hold the line low (e.g. during reset) without fighting another device trying to drive it high.

2. Level Shifting / Mixed Voltage Buses

Because the open-drain output never drives high itself, the pull-up resistor can be connected to a different voltage rail than the device's own supply. A 3.3 V device with an open-drain output can sit on a 5 V I²C bus (pull-up to 5 V) as long as its pins tolerate 5 V when "off" — check the datasheet for VIH tolerance.

3. Fault / Status Outputs

Many ICs (voltage regulators, motor drivers, battery chargers) use an open-drain "fault" or "ready" pin so it can be wired directly into a shared status line with other devices, or pulled up to whatever logic level the rest of the system uses.

Speed Limitations

The falling edge is fast — the transistor actively pulls the line down. The rising edge is passive — it depends entirely on the pull-up resistor charging the line's parasitic capacitance.

trise ≈ 0.69 × Rpullup × Cbus  (time to reach 50% of VCC)

This asymmetry is why open-drain buses (like I²C) have firm maximum speed specifications tied to the pull-up value and bus capacitance.

Practical tip If an I²C bus works fine with one or two devices but becomes unreliable as more devices are added, suspect the rising edge first. Each device adds a few pF of pin capacitance; total bus capacitance climbs, and the same pull-up resistor now produces a slower, more rounded rising edge that may violate setup-time requirements at high clock speeds.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the pull-up entirely: the line will float when released, reading inconsistently as high or low depending on noise.
  • Tying an open-drain output directly to a push-pull input expecting a strong driver: works electrically (the pull-up provides the high), but if the pull-up is too weak for the input's leakage or capacitance, the "high" level may sag.
  • Mixing open-drain and push-pull on the same shared line: a push-pull device actively driving high while an open-drain device pulls low creates a direct short through the push-pull device's high-side transistor — potentially damaging it.
  • Assuming open-drain outputs can sink unlimited current: the pull-down transistor has a maximum sink current rating (often tens of mA) — don't use it to drive an LED or relay directly without checking.
